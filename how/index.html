How it works

No jargon, no hand-waving. If any answer here does not satisfy you, that is a problem with the design rather than with you.

Where does my money actually go?

Nowhere. That is the whole design.

You keep your bitcoin under your own key the whole time. There is no account to pay into and no pot. You lock your own coins: you send them to a new address that only your key can open, with a rule that stops even you spending them before a date you pick. That rule is called a timelock. The money is still yours. It is just out of reach for a while, and anyone can see that it is.

So there is nothing for an organiser to run off with. Not because we are trustworthy, but because we never have it.

Why would I lock my own money up?

Because otherwise the number means nothing.

A total of bitcoin that supporters merely own would only show how much they happen to hold. No club owner, journalist or solicitor would take it seriously. A locked coin is money somebody has put beyond their own reach on purpose, and anyone can check that against the blockchain.

You pick the length: one year, three or five. Longer is a louder statement.

What happens when the lock runs out?

Your coins become spendable by you again, and nothing else happens.

Nobody has to agree. There is no form, no notice period and no permission to ask. On your date your own signature is enough. If this movement has collapsed, or the people running the register have vanished, your money still comes home on the day you picked.

It also stops counting towards the total that day, whether you have moved it or not. Money you could spend tomorrow is not a commitment.

If nobody holds the money, how does anything ever get bought?

Your locked coins can leave in two directions, and no others.

Home to you: your signature alone, on or after your date.

Into a purchase: your signature plus a second one, at any time.

When there is something to buy, each supporter commits their own locked money to that purchase. Your commitment is your payment. The money never sits in a fund waiting, so there is no moment when it could be taken, misdirected or lost.

Nothing anybody else does can move your money, so nobody is ever outvoted.

So how is it decided what to buy? Do we vote on it?

No. There is nothing to vote on.

A proposal is published: what it buys, what it costs, and the address the money goes to. Supporters who want it sign their locked money into it. Supporters who don't, do nothing. If enough sign in time, it goes through. If not, it doesn't.

So there is no ballot, quorum or turnout. Funding is the decision.

A proposal has up to four weeks to gather support, then up to two to go through. That is short on purpose: the lock is the long commitment, and backing one purchase is a brief last step. Four weeks is a limit, not a wait. The moment enough money is behind a proposal, gathering stops and it starts going through. Nothing is sent until the whole amount is there, so money never reaches a seller for a purchase that doesn't happen.

While a proposal is open your commitment is binding. You can't un-sign it, so don't sign for something you are not sure about.

Under the rules anyone may put a proposal up, and a bad one simply gets no support. Today a proposal only appears once it is added to the fund's published list, and one person keeps that list. What stops a fake one is separate: the address it pays must already be on a published list of approved destinations. That list has its own document in the documents.

Who holds that second signature, and what can they do with it?

Less than you would think, and that is deliberate. Right now it is one supporter, whose key is published, and here is the exact extent of it:

They cannot take your money. Your own signature is needed alongside theirs, every time, for every coin.

They cannot keep your money. The route by which your lock comes home does not involve them at all. They could lose the key, change their mind or disappear, and your coins would still return to you on your date.

They can refuse to sign. That is the whole of their power. It would cost the movement an opportunity. It cannot cost you a penny.

Which is worth saying plainly for a reason that is not modesty: that key is worthless to a thief. It cannot move money on its own, so stealing it, or threatening its holder for it, would get somebody nothing at all.

It is meant to stop being one person. The plan is to split it so that two of three holders must agree, and in time to hand it to the society as a duty. Splitting it now would change nothing, because the keys would all be in the same house. What makes it real is other people holding them. Holding a share needs a phone and twelve words written on paper: nothing to buy and nothing to run, so the job can be spread without anybody being paid or out of pocket.

What do I get out of it?

A say. Never money.

If this ever owns a stake in Liverpool, a supporters' society will hold it, and its own rules will force it to vote the club's shares the way its members vote. Not consult them: vote as they voted, on the public record, so anyone can check.

Two kinds of decision work differently. Spending money isn't voted on: you fund what you want funded, so nobody can out-vote the people putting the money up. Directing the club is one member, one vote, because a wealthy supporter is not more of a supporter, and because a question like "should the club back this stadium plan" moves no money, so funding can't settle it. The plan for the society is here.

Why bitcoin? Why not just a bank account?

Because a bank account has somebody's name on it.

Every pooled fund needs a person or company to hold it, and that is where it fails: they can be pressured, sued, mistaken or dishonest. Bitcoin is the only money that lets a hundred thousand strangers commit to the same thing while each keeps their own, provably, with nobody trusted in the middle.

It also lets anyone anywhere join without paperwork, which matters when the club's supporters are scattered across the world.

The cost is real: bitcoin's price moves, sometimes brutally, and while your money is locked you can do nothing about it. It is the first thing on the risks page.

Do I have to use your app or your website?

No, and that is a rule, not a courtesy.

This movement is a set of published documents, not a piece of software. Anyone can write their own app from them, or use an ordinary bitcoin wallet and no app at all, and the register has to treat them the same. Anyone can also run their own copy of the register. The page reads one public file and checks it in your own browser, so if your total ever disagrees with ours, ours is wrong.

A signature cannot be forged and needs nobody's permission to pass on. If we ever refused to publish yours, you could hand it to anybody who would.

What if the person running this gets hit by a bus?

Your money is unaffected. It comes home on its date with nobody's help.

The movement is a harder question. It is one person at the moment, and that is a weakness. What helps is that everything is published: the declaration, the specifications, the code and the record of every change. Somebody else could pick it up without needing anything that exists only in one person's head.

What is built, what is still open, and what we got wrong along the way are on one page: where this stands.
